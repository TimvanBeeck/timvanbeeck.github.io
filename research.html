01

Acoustic waves in nematic Korteweg fluids

My PhD thesis studies time-harmonic acoustic waves in nematic liquid crystals, building on the work of Farrell and Zerbinati.

Why does the alignment of the molecules change the waves? Show explanation

In an ordinary fluid, the molecules point in random directions and keep tumbling around, so no direction is special. A sound wave from a point source therefore spreads equally fast in all directions, and its wavefronts are circles.

A nematic liquid crystal is still a fluid: its rod-like molecules move and wobble freely. On average, however, they line up along a common direction, the director n. This order makes the fluid respond differently to compression along n than across it, so sound travels faster along the director and the wavefronts become ellipses stretched along n. Turning the director turns the ellipses with it, which is what retunes the drum above.

Sound that can be steered

An external electric field can reorient the director n, so the acoustic response of a nematic fluid can be changed without changing the device that holds it. This makes nematic liquid crystals candidates for tunable acoustic devices. In the slit ring shown here, rotating the director decides whether the same incoming wave is trapped inside the ring or simply scatters off it.

I study the equations that describe these waves. For a constant director, the density perturbation satisfies the nematic Helmholtz–Korteweg (NHK) equation, a fourth-order extension of the Helmholtz equation with an isotropic and an anisotropic leading-order term. For vanishing anisotropy, it reduces to the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation for isotropic capillary fluids, such as liquid–vapor mixtures or quantum fluids, to which the results also apply.

Analysis and discretization of the NHK equation

We proved well-posedness of the NHK equation and devised its first convergent discretization, based on C1-conforming elements. The proof constructs T-coercivity from the eigenbasis of the isotropic problem and treats the anisotropy as a perturbation, so the admissible anisotropy vanishes near isotropic resonances.

In follow-up work, I removed this dependence by quantifying the anisotropy through the Cordes condition, which yields a Gårding inequality under a smallness condition on the anisotropy alone. Since C1 elements are hard to construct on curved or three-dimensional domains, I also introduced a hybrid C0-interior penalty discretization that uses standard Lagrange finite elements and whose cost is reduced by static condensation.

02

Structure-preserving finite element methods

Another central theme of my work is to devise structure-preserving discretizations that are exactly divergence-free, ensuring mass conservation and, in turn, pressure-robustness for incompressible flow problems. We have developed such methods for axisymmetric problems and unfitted meshes, where standard constructions fail, and for surface flows, where the topology of the surface plays a crucial role.

Pressure-robustness for the axisymmetric Stokes problem

We developed the first pressure-robust discretization of the axisymmetric Stokes problem on general meshes. In engineering, problems posed on rotationally symmetric 3D geometries are routinely reduced to 2D problems by a Fourier expansion in the azimuthal direction. Since the axisymmetric divergence contains the additional term ur/r, methods that are pressure-robust in Cartesian coordinates (e.g., Scott–Vogelius or H(div)-conforming methods) lose this property, and the 2D reduction can cost more than solving in 3D.

The key idea is a velocity reconstruction in the right-hand side, built from standard H(div)-conforming interpolation operators modified locally at the symmetry axis, that restores it for the low-order Bernardi–Raugel element.

How does the reduction to 2D work? Show explanation

A rotationally symmetric domain is what you get by rotating a 2D cross-section, the meridian domain, once around the symmetry axis. In cylindrical coordinates (r, θ, z), functions on the 3D domain can be expanded in Fourier modes in the angle θ, and each mode solves its own problem on the 2D meridian domain. If the data do not depend on θ, a single 2D problem suffices.

Every point (r, z) of the meridian domain stands for a whole circle in 3D, so the 2D problem is much smaller. The price is a change in the equations: integrals carry the weight r, the divergence becomes ∂rur + ur/r + ∂zuz, and the symmetry axis r = 0, where the weight vanishes, becomes part of the boundary of the 2D domain.

Mass-conserving unfitted finite elements

We proposed and analyzed an unfitted mixed finite element method that is robust to small cuts and conserves mass exactly. Unfitted methods decouple the geometry from the computational mesh to handle complex or evolving geometries, but small cut elements usually require stabilization terms, and these pollute the mass balance.

The key idea is to formulate the divergence constraint on the active mesh, i.e., on all elements that touch the domain. The resulting slight inconsistency does not affect the flux, and a local post-processing of the scalar variable recovers optimal rates.

What are unfitted finite elements? Show explanation

Standard finite element methods need a mesh whose elements line up with the boundary of the domain. For complex geometries, generating such a mesh is expensive, and when the geometry moves or deforms, the mesh has to be deformed or generated again.

Unfitted methods instead use one fixed background mesh and describe the domain implicitly, for instance as the region where a level set function is negative. The boundary may cut through elements anywhere. Integrals on a cut element are computed only over the part inside the domain, and boundary conditions are imposed weakly instead of through the mesh.

The price is that this part can be arbitrarily small, which makes standard discretizations unstable and ill-conditioned. The usual fix adds stabilization terms near the boundary; in mixed methods, these disturb the discrete mass balance, which our method avoids.

Incompressible flows on surfaces of any topology

We introduced discretizations of incompressible flows on surfaces of any topology that avoid the saddle-point structure of standard mixed methods. On simply connected surfaces, where all divergence-free fields are rotated gradients, a streamfunction formulation achieves this. On general surfaces, the divergence-free fields also contain harmonic fields, which a streamfunction cannot capture.

The method therefore adds a precomputed basis of discrete harmonic fields to the streamfunction. A discrete Helmholtz–Hodge decomposition shows that this splitting captures all discretely divergence-free velocities. The resulting high-order method is pressure-robust by design, with only a streamfunction and finitely many harmonic coefficients as unknowns.

Why does the topology matter? Show explanation

On a surface, a divergence-free velocity can often be written as the rotated gradient of a scalar streamfunction. The flow then follows the level lines of the streamfunction, and using it as the unknown satisfies incompressibility automatically, without a pressure.

This works on simply connected surfaces, such as the Stanford bunny. A surface with handles or holes also carries harmonic fields: divergence-free velocities that circulate around the handles and cannot be written with a streamfunction. How many there are depends only on the topology; a closed surface with g handles has 2g of them. Punch a hole through the bunny and two appear.

Numerical methods for helioseismology

Hybrid discontinuous Galerkin methods for Galbrun's equation

We developed hybrid discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) discretizations of Galbrun's equation that are stable and optimally convergent for all polynomial degrees. Galbrun's equation is a vector-valued time-harmonic wave equation that models stellar oscillations and is central to helioseismology, the study of the Sun's interior through its surface oscillations.

Inferring the interior structure requires many efficient and accurate forward solves, which is challenging because the equation is highly indefinite and its coefficients vary over several orders of magnitude between the core and the surface. Its analysis relies on weak T-coercivity: a generalized Helmholtz decomposition allows one to flip the sign of the divergence-free part, which yields a coercive problem up to a compact perturbation.

Discretely, this requires a stable divergence operator which, as for the Stokes problem, restricts H1-conforming methods to high polynomial degrees or special meshes. The central ingredient is a discrete Helmholtz decomposition of the DG spaces, which, combined with suitably weighted norms, yields stability and robustness with respect to the coefficients. Hybridization mitigates the cost of a non-conforming discretization.
